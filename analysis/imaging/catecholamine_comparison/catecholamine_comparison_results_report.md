# Persistence, spatial confinement and anatomical organisation of catecholamine-reporter signals in LC-CA1 axons

Updated 31 August 2026.

## Chapter conclusion

This chapter begins with the experimentally constrained result, because that is where the distinction between the reporters is clearest: following LC stimulation, nLightG fluorescence decayed more slowly than dLight3.6 fluorescence among sessions that met the prespecified fit criterion, and the separation became larger rather than smaller when each measured decay constant was divided by the published off-kinetic of its reporter. The same stimulation fields then showed a second, spatial distinction. dLight3.6 responses were detected in most labelled LC-axon ROIs and decayed over a longer pixel-domain distance, whereas nLightG responses were detected in a much smaller ROI fraction and were more tightly confined around labelled axons.

Natural run-onset events provide the necessary change of context. They recruit both reporter cohorts, with the apparent cohort difference in responsive fraction depending on the classifier setting, but they do not reproduce the stimulation-evoked decay separation: neither natural-event decay nor rise time differs detectably between cohorts. The chapter therefore does not claim that norepinephrine itself has a universally longer decay, or that the reporters are interchangeable during behaviour. It establishes that the prolonged nLightG trace is a robust property of the fit-qualified optogenetic recordings, while making clear that the recorded trace contains transmitter dynamics, reporter kinetics, expression, optical sampling and fit selection.

The final section asks whether vesicles are uniformly arranged within four reconstructed axonal profiles. Against profile-specific nulls, vesicles are more tightly clustered within mitochondria-excluded cytoplasm in every profile, after which proximity to nearby synapse centres and unequal vesicle loading distinguish some profiles but not others. This supplies anatomical evidence for local organisation without pretending that four profiles are four animals, that nearby synapse centres are proven contacts, or that the ultrastructure explains the optical reporter difference.

## Results

### LC stimulation evokes a more persistent nLightG fluorescence trace

We first compared axonal fluorescence following LC stimulation, for which event timing, pulse delivery and the acquisition blank were experimentally defined. Figure 18 opens with the same objectively selected dLight3.6 and nLightG fields throughout the stimulation analysis: `A152i-20251020-02` and `A171i-20260220-01`, respectively. Reference images locate the labelled axons, continuous 0-4 s Delta F/F maps show the measured response before thresholding, and ROI outlines make the sampling geometry visible. Each trial was re-zeroed to its own prestimulation baseline; the acquisition blank remains missing data rather than being interpolated.

The two whole-field stimulation traces are shown together from 0 to 10 s after shutter reopening, with dLight3.6 in green and nLightG in blue. Within each trial, time zero is the first finite post-stimulation frame, which is the first moment at which the shutter has reopened and a reporter signal is observed; the cohort line and shading are then the mean +/- SEM across 32 and 18 independently aligned trials. The source arrays remain unchanged for the downstream analyses. The response-qualified ROI traces in the same fields are then unit-normalised only for the direct comparison of trace shape and fitted decay.

Among fit-qualified sessions, nLightG decay was 1.917 [1.694, 2.070] s across 8 sessions from 3 animals, compared with 1.366 [1.290, 1.432] s across 34 sessions from 4 animals. The session comparison gave U = 262, p = 2.30 x 10^-6 and rank-biserial correlation = .926; the exact comparison of animal medians gave p = .0571. This is strong and highly consistent separation among sessions, with the small animal-level test correctly showing the limit on population inference.

Two controls sharpen that interpretation. First, the fit-independent 0-4 s persistence index was 1.412 s for nLightG and 1.260 s for dLight3.6 before neuropil subtraction, and 1.431 and 1.089 s after subtraction. Neuropil subtraction changed the dLight3.6 distribution (paired p = 2.95 x 10^-8) but not the nLightG distribution (paired p = .839), so the longer nLightG trace is not created by the surrounding neuropil signal. Second, dividing the fitted decay by the published reporter off-kinetic, 0.33 s for dLight3.6 and 0.19 s for nLightG, yielded medians of 4.081 [3.855, 4.279] and 9.881 [8.734, 10.671], respectively. The session comparison gave U = 272, p = 1.69 x 10^-8 and rank-biserial correlation = 1.0; the animal-level exact p value was .0571. The measured separation therefore cannot be explained by the published off-kinetics alone, although this normalisation does not remove expression, acquisition or fit selection and is not an *in vivo* concentration calibration.

The difference in fit yield still bounds the conclusion: all 34 included dLight3.6 time courses yielded a decay fit, whereas 8 of 13 included nLightG time courses did. The paper-facing claim should therefore remain: 'LC stimulation evoked a more prolonged nLightG fluorescence trace than dLight3.6 among recordings meeting the prespecified decay-fit criterion.'

### Stimulation-evoked nLightG responses are more spatially confined

Having established the temporal difference, we next applied the Chapter 2 spatial procedure to both cohorts rather than inferring spatial organisation from response-weight summaries. Figure 19 shows response-index maps for the same representative fields as Figure 18, with the labelled LC-axon ROIs overlaid, then measures response index in one-pixel rings extending away from those axons. Each session contributes one ring profile and the plotted cohort curves are mean +/- SEM across sessions. Both curves decay with distance, but the nLightG profile begins lower and falls more steeply.

The fitted spatial decay constant was 3.804 [3.339, 4.392] pixels for nLightG across 13 sessions from 4 animals and 5.856 [4.719, 8.545] pixels for dLight3.6 across 34 sessions from 4 animals. The session comparison gave U = 70, p = 3.45 x 10^-4 and rank-biserial correlation = -.683; the exact animal-level p value was .200. This is a session-level difference in native image pixels, not yet a physical length-scale estimate.

Response qualification provides the complementary result. The implemented Chapter 2 criterion is a one-sample greater-than-zero test together with mean response index above 0.1; it is not a stimulation-shuffle classifier. The median qualifying fraction was .0938 [.0476, .250] for nLightG and .918 [.850, 1.000] for dLight3.6. The session comparison gave U = 4, p = 2.19 x 10^-7 and rank-biserial correlation = -.982, while the exact animal-level comparison gave p = .0286. Thus, under the same implemented rule, the nLightG stimulation response is detected in fewer labelled axonal ROIs and, among the sessions entering the spatial analysis, over a shorter fitted pixel-domain reach.

This distinction is about reporter-defined recruitment and spatial response, not a direct count of release sites. Different reporter sensitivity, expression and signal-to-noise can affect which ROIs cross the common criterion, while the field geometries do not support a single physical conversion for every recording. The conclusion is therefore spatial confinement of the measured response, not a claim that norepinephrine release has an intrinsically smaller biological radius.

### Natural run-onset events do not reproduce the optogenetic decay separation

We then asked whether the stimulation-evoked distinction was also apparent at a natural transition into running. Figure 20 uses 57 dLight3.6 sessions from 4 animals and 26 nLightG sessions from 2 animals. Within each session, the median field response was calculated first; cohort traces are then mean +/- SEM across sessions. Representative maps use the primary natural-event classifier, in which response must exceed the 95th percentile of a circular-shuffle distribution and an effect-size threshold of .05. This circular shuffle is specific to natural-event classification and should not be described as the Figure 19 stimulation criterion.

At the primary setting, the median responsive fraction was .103 for nLightG and .195 for dLight3.6. The session-level comparison gave U = 437.5, p = .00293 and rank-biserial correlation = -.410, but the exact animal-level p value was .533. Across the full nine-setting grid, the session contrast is visible at the 90th-percentile settings with effect thresholds of 0 or .05, and at the corresponding 95th-percentile settings, but it disappears at the stricter effect threshold and at the 99th-percentile settings; no animal-level exact comparison is significant. The appropriate conclusion is that apparent field recruitment depends on how a natural response is operationalised, especially with only two nLightG animals.

The natural-event kinetic results differ from the stimulation result. Natural decay was 1.063 [0.615, 1.810] s for nLightG across 22 sessions from 2 animals and 1.339 [0.927, 1.649] s for dLight3.6 across 44 sessions from 4 animals, with session p = .698 and animal p = 1.000. Rise time was .850 [0.375, 1.996] s and 1.017 [0.663, 1.800] s, respectively, with session p = .549 and animal p = .533. These non-significant comparisons do not prove equal kinetics; they show that the optogenetic nLightG persistence difference is not detected in the available run-onset analysis.

Baseline fluorescence, pre-onset noise, usable trial count and motion-regression fit are displayed alongside the natural-event endpoints because each can change apparent sensitivity or fit quality. Their purpose is to make cohort comparability inspectable, not to manufacture a single nuisance-corrected score; the red reference channel is not plotted in the publication figure.

### Reconstructed axonal profiles contain heterogeneous vesicle organisation near nearby synapse centres

The final analysis moves from reporter fluorescence to serial-section ultrastructure acquired with the MPFI EM Core. the EM panel set shows all four reconstructed profiles separately, including the axon mask, mitochondrial exclusion, vesicle coordinates and the centres of nearby neuropil synapses within the fixed 1,000-nm inclusion distance. P1-P4 contain 106, 76, 100 and 22 annotated vesicles across 32, 15, 28 and 41 sections, respectively; the corresponding numbers of included nearby synapse centres are 7, 5, 34 and 24.

We first asked the more basic question, before invoking synapses, of whether vesicles were uniformly dispersed through the cytoplasm that was actually available to them. The observed median nearest-neighbour distances were 52.0, 39.1, 57.2 and 56.7 nm in P1-P4, compared with null medians of 73.1, 62.5, 105.1 and 149.1 nm after mitochondrial volume was excluded. Every profile therefore showed tighter vesicle clustering than any but the most extreme of 500 profile-specific null patterns (empirical p = .001996 and BH-adjusted q = .001996 in each profile). This common result establishes subcellular clustering without assuming that mitochondrial space was available for vesicles.

For each profile, one observed median vesicle-to-nearest-synapse-centre distance was compared with 500 deterministic patterns sampled uniformly from the segmented cytoplasm with mitochondria excluded. P1 and P2 had shorter observed medians than their nulls: 428.9 versus 573.9 nm and 290.8 versus 371.4 nm, both p = .00200 and BH-adjusted q = .00399. P3 and P4 did not: 620.6 versus 609.7 nm, p = .623, and 462.6 versus 440.4 nm, p = .723. The proximity result is therefore profile-specific rather than universal.

The coefficient of variation of synapse-assigned vesicle counts asks a related question about unequal loading. P1, P2 and P4 exceeded their null expectations, with p = .00200, .0339 and .0220 and adjusted q = .00798, .0452 and .0439, respectively; P3 did not, p = .631. Together, the two statistics show organised vesicle pools in some reconstructed profiles, while the discordant profiles and the absence of an animal-level sampling structure rule out a general population claim.

Two requested controls cannot be calculated from the supplied inputs. Vesicle-radius erosion requires feasible-centre masks that are not present, and annotation agreement requires an independent annotation set that has not been supplied. These constraints remain part of the inference boundary, although panels that merely reported their unavailability have been removed. The null consequently remains uniform-in-mask sampling with replacement, and the synapse coordinates must be called 'nearby neuropil synapse centres', not confirmed synapses made by the reconstructed axon or identified release sites.

## Methods and inference hierarchy

Animals are the biological replicates and sessions are nested observations. Distribution panels show sessions to expose heterogeneity and fit selection, but cross-cohort statistics report both a two-sided session-level Mann-Whitney U test and an exact test on animal medians. A non-significant animal-level result is treated as limited power, not equivalence. Natural-event classifier sensitivity is shown across every prespecified threshold combination rather than selected after inspection. EM inference is restricted to one observed statistic per profile against one statistic from each null pattern; simulated vesicles are never treated as independent biological replicates.

The assembly output directory contains only the chapter panel set in PNG, PDF and SVG formats, while `data/catecholamine_comparison/representative_inputs/panel_source_table.csv`, `summary_statistics.csv`, `persistence_statistics.csv` and `em_profile_null_statistics.csv` provide the panel-level numerical sources.


### Observation-clock alignment for the optogenetic traces

The representative whole-field traces in Figure 18c are aligned independently for each trial to the first finite frame after the shutter reopens. Baseline subtraction uses the preceding one second, and the mean with SEM is calculated only over complete trial traces; this avoids a progressively shrinking n at the right edge and prevents the next stimulation from entering the displayed tail.
